\begin{abstract}
This report documents my work on the QRT $\times$ Institut Gustave Roussy data
challenge on Challenge Data (ENS Paris), whose goal is to rank adult patients with
myeloid leukemia by their risk of death, using clinical measurements, cytogenetic
reports and somatic gene mutations. Models are scored with the inverse probability
of censoring weighted (IPCW) concordance index truncated at seven years. The data
audit shows that the 1{,}193 test patients all come from a single clinical centre that
is absent from the 23 training centres, and that this centre's patients are clinically
sicker, so model validation will hold out whole centres. Kaplan--Meier analysis gives a
median overall survival of 3.15 years, with strong separation by bone-marrow blasts,
\textit{TP53} and \textit{SF3B1} mutations and monosomy~7 (log-rank $p<0.001$ for all).
The Kaplan--Meier estimator, a log-rank test and both concordance indices were computed
by hand and match standard software. Single raw variables reach an IPCW C-index of at
most 0.616. I then built 91 features from blood counts, a forgiving parser of the
free-text karyotypes, and somatic mutations restricted to the 64 genes sequenced in both
centres. The published IPSS-R clinical risk score, which needs no fitting, reaches an
IPCW C-index of 0.669 $\pm$ 0.029 across 15 centre-grouped validation folds. Cox models
evaluated on the same folds improve on it: a 24-variable model built from the IPSS-M
gene list reaches 0.716 and an elastic-net Cox model on 84 features 0.722, each beating
the IPSS-R on all 15 folds. Monocyte counts, missing in 74\% of test patients, add
nothing. Hazard ratios agree with clinical knowledge (TP53 multi-hit HR 3.2), and the
proportional-hazards assumption is violated mainly by \textit{NPM1} and platelets, whose
effects change over time. Tree models add little: a random survival forest reaches 0.719,
and XGBoost with a Cox loss, tuned by nested cross-validation and averaged over five
seeds, reaches 0.724, a small but consistent gain (14 of 15 folds). A rank average of the
elastic-net and XGBoost reaches 0.725 and beats the elastic-net on all 15 folds. Chosen
as the final model before its public score was known, it scored 0.7552 on the unseen
test centre, the best of all submissions, while XGBoost alone fell to 0.7504. Permutation
importance on held-out centres shows that the mutation burden, counted on the gene panel
shared by both centres, is the strongest signal. Against the published IPSS-M score,
computed with no fitting (cross-validated 0.720), no fitted model is statistically better
in cross-validation (ensemble $+0.005$, 95\% CI $-0.005$ to $+0.014$), and the recalibrated
IPSS-M is slightly better calibrated. On the unseen test centre, however, the IPSS-M scored
only 0.7255, 0.030 below the ensemble. Neither the test centre's sicker case mix nor its
missing blood counts explains this gap; it lies above the range seen across training
centres ($-0.014$ to $+0.019$), but it rests on one centre and has no confidence interval.
Domain knowledge, whether built into the features or into the IPSS-M, accounts for nearly
all of the predictive performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ==================================================================
Myeloid leukemias are blood cancers in which abnormal immature cells (blasts)
accumulate in the bone marrow. Estimating a patient's prognosis guides treatment:
low-risk patients may receive supportive care, while high-risk patients may be
prioritised for intensive treatment such as stem cell transplantation.

The outcome is \emph{overall survival} (OS): the time from diagnosis to death or to the
last follow-up. When a patient is still alive at the last follow-up, the true survival
time is unknown; we only know it is longer than the observed time. Such observations
are called \emph{right-censored}, and handling them correctly is the purpose of
survival analysis.

The project follows a staged plan:
Kaplan--Meier estimation and log-rank tests,
Cox proportional hazards regression,
and machine-learning survival models (gradient-boosted survival and random survival
forests), all compared under the challenge metric with honest cross-validation.
A similar crowdsourced effort, the DREAM 9 AML Outcome Prediction Challenge, also
scored survival models with a concordance index~\cite{noren2016}.

\section{The evaluation metric}
\label{sec:metric}
% ==================================================================
For each patient $i$ we observe a time $T_i$ (\texttt{OS\_YEARS}), an event indicator
$\Delta_i$ (1 = death, 0 = censored), and the model gives a risk score $\eta_i$.

\paragraph{Harrell's C-index.}
A pair of patients $(i,j)$ is \emph{comparable} when we know who died first, that is,
when $T_i < T_j$ and patient $i$ died ($\Delta_i = 1$). The pair is \emph{concordant}
when the model agrees, $\eta_i > \eta_j$. Harrell's C-index~\cite{harrell1982} is
\begin{equation}
C = \frac{\text{number of concordant comparable pairs}}{\text{number of comparable pairs}}.
\label{eq:harrell}
\end{equation}
A value of $0.5$ corresponds to random guessing and $1$ to a perfect ranking. Pairs
with equal risk scores count as one half.

\paragraph{IPCW C-index (Uno's C).}
Patients who die late are less likely to be observed dying, because many patients are
censored before then. Harrell's C therefore over-represents early deaths. Uno
\textit{et al.}~\cite{uno2011} correct this by weighting each comparable pair by
$1/\hat G(T_i)^2$, where $\hat G(t)$ is the Kaplan--Meier estimate of the probability
of \emph{not yet being censored} at time $t$. With the truncation horizon $\tau = 7$
years used by the challenge,
\begin{equation}
C_\tau = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{j} \Delta_i\,\hat G(T_i)^{-2}\,
\mathbf{1}(T_i < T_j,\ T_i < \tau)\,\mathbf{1}(\eta_i > \eta_j)}
{\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{j} \Delta_i\,\hat G(T_i)^{-2}\,
\mathbf{1}(T_i < T_j,\ T_i < \tau)}.
\label{eq:uno}
\end{equation}
In plain language: a death at a time when few patients remain uncensored is rare to
observe, so it counts more. Only the ordering of the scores $\eta_i$ matters. The
challenge computes $C_\tau$ with \texttt{scikit-survival}~\cite{polsterl2020}.

\subsection{The Kaplan--Meier estimator}
Let $t_1 < t_2 < \dots$ be the distinct times at which at least one death occurs. At
each $t_k$ let $n_k$ be the number of patients still \emph{at risk} just before $t_k$
(neither dead nor censored yet) and $d_k$ the number of deaths at $t_k$. The
Kaplan--Meier estimator~\cite{kaplan1958} of the survival function is
\begin{equation}
\hat S(t) = \prod_{k:\ t_k \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_k}{n_k}\right).
\label{eq:km}
\end{equation}
In plain language: to survive past $t_k$ you must survive every earlier death time,
and at each one the chance of surviving, given you were still alive, is the share of
the at-risk patients who did not die. Censored patients never make the curve drop;
they simply leave the risk set, so they count fully for as long as they were observed.

Table~\ref{tab:kmsteps} shows the first six steps. The first death occurs on day one
($t = 0.00274$ years) with $n = 3{,}120$ patients at risk: this is $3{,}173 - 53$, because
the 53 patients with an observed time of zero were all censored at that time and left
the risk set immediately. The first two steps are
\begin{align*}
\hat S(0.00274) &= 1 - \tfrac{1}{3120} = 0.99968,\\
\hat S(0.00822) &= 0.99968 \times \left(1 - \tfrac{2}{3114}\right)
                 = 0.99968 \times 0.99936 = 0.99904.
\end{align*}
My hand implementation of Equation~\eqref{eq:km} agrees with the \texttt{lifelines}
library~\cite{lifelines2019} at every death time to within $2\times 10^{-14}$.

\subsection{Comparing groups: the log-rank test}
To ask whether two groups have different survival, the log-rank
test~\cite{mantel1966} compares, at each death time, the deaths observed in group~1
with the deaths it would receive if the groups were identical. With $n$ at risk in
total, $n_1$ in group~1, $n_2 = n - n_1$, $d$ deaths in total and $d_1$ in group~1,
\begin{equation}
E_{1k} = d\,\frac{n_1}{n}, \qquad
V_k = \frac{n_1\, n_2\, d\,(n - d)}{n^2 (n-1)}, \qquad
\chi^2 = \frac{\left(\sum_k (d_{1k} - E_{1k})\right)^2}{\sum_k V_k},
\label{eq:logrank}
\end{equation}
which follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom when the groups do not
differ. In plain language: group~1 should get ``its fair share'' $n_1/n$ of the deaths
at each time; $\chi^2$ measures how far the total gap is from zero, in units of its own
variability.

\paragraph{Worked example: \textit{TP53}.}
Summing over all death times, the 368 patients with a \textit{TP53} mutation had
$O_1 = 271$ observed deaths against $E_1 = 112.13$ expected, with total variance
$V = 103.17$. Hence
\[
\chi^2 = \frac{(271 - 112.13)^2}{103.17} = \frac{158.87^2}{103.17} = 244.64,
\qquad p = 3.8\times 10^{-55},
\]
identical to the \texttt{lifelines} result. \textit{TP53}-mutated patients died more
than twice as often as expected under ``no difference''.

\subsection{The metric by hand: a six-patient example}
Figure~\ref{fig:toy} shows six patients with observed times, statuses and risk scores.

\paragraph{Comparable pairs.} Only deaths can start a comparable pair, and only with
patients observed for longer. Patient~A (death at 1) is comparable with B, C, D, E, F;
patient~C (death at 3) with D, E, F; patient~E (death at 5) with F; patient~F (death at
8) has nobody after it. That gives $5 + 3 + 1 = 9$ comparable pairs. Of these, two are
discordant: $(C, D)$ because $0.60 < 0.80$ and $(E, F)$ because $0.05 < 0.10$. So
\[
C_{\text{Harrell}} = \frac{7}{9} = 0.778.
\]

\paragraph{Censoring curve.} Censorings occur at $t=2$ (B), with 5 patients at risk,
and at $t = 4$ (D), with 3 at risk:
\[
\hat G(2) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.8, \qquad
\hat G(4) = 0.8 \times \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = 0.5333.
\]
So $\hat G(1) = 1$, $\hat G(3) = 0.8$ and $\hat G(5) = 0.5333$, giving the weights
\[
w_A = \frac{1}{1^2} = 1, \qquad
w_C = \frac{1}{0.8^2} = 1.5625, \qquad
w_E = \frac{1}{0.5333^2} = 3.5156.
\]
Patient F died at $8 \ge \tau = 7$ and starts no pair anyway.

\paragraph{IPCW C-index.}
\begin{align*}
\text{numerator}   &= 5\times 1 + 2 \times 1.5625 + 0 \times 3.5156 = 8.1250,\\
\text{denominator} &= 5\times 1 + 3 \times 1.5625 + 1 \times 3.5156 = 13.2031,\\
C_7 &= \frac{8.1250}{13.2031} = 0.615.
\end{align*}
Both values match \texttt{scikit-survival} exactly. The IPCW value is lower because both
wrong pairs start from later deaths (C at 3 years, E at 5 years), whose weights are
larger.

\subsection{Clinical features}
Blood counts are strongly right-skewed (Figure~\ref{fig:dist}), so white cells,

\subsection{Parsing the karyotype}
A karyotype such as \texttt{46,xy,del(20)(q12)[2]/46,xy[18]} lists clones separated by
``\texttt{/}'', each with a chromosome count, the sex chromosomes, the abnormalities and
a cell count in square brackets. Numerical changes are written \texttt{-7} (loss of
chromosome~7) or \texttt{+8} (an extra chromosome~8); structural changes as
\texttt{del}, \texttt{t}, \texttt{inv}, \texttt{der}, \texttt{add}, \texttt{i} and so on,
and unidentified marker chromosomes as \texttt{mar}.

The text is irregular, especially in the test centre: upper and lower case, spaces,
\texttt{\{16\}} or \texttt{[3/20]} cell counts, unbalanced brackets, and free text such as
\texttt{Normal}, \texttt{complex} or \texttt{No mitosis}. The parser therefore searches for
patterns instead of fully splitting the string:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item lower-case the text, remove spaces, and convert \texttt{\{\}} to \texttt{[]};
  \item map free text: \texttt{Normal} to a normal karyotype, \texttt{complex} to at least
        three abnormalities, \texttt{>3 abnormalities} to four, and \texttt{No mitosis} or
        \texttt{Not evaluated} to missing;
  \item delete cell counts, then collect the distinct numerical changes, the distinct
        structural changes and any marker chromosome across all clones;
  \item do not count a translocation that only describes how a derivative chromosome
        was formed (\texttt{der(1)t(1;7)} is one abnormality), nor the common harmless
        variant \texttt{inv(9)(p12q13)}.
\end{enumerate}
The parser was checked on 25 hand-written test strings covering these cases, and its
monosomy~7 count (165 patients) agrees exactly with the simpler rule used in Stage~2.
From the parsed karyotype the patient is assigned an IPSS-R cytogenetic
category~\cite{schanz2012} (Table~\ref{tab:cytocat}).

\subsection{Centre-grouped cross-validation}
\label{sec:cv}
All patients of one centre are placed in the same fold, so each validation fold contains
only centres unseen during training. The 23 centres are dealt into $K = 5$ folds like
cards: they are put in a random order, and each in turn goes to the fold that currently
has the fewest patients, which keeps the folds of similar size. Because there are only 23
centres, the split is repeated three times with different random orders, giving 15
validation folds. Two checks run in the code: no centre is split across folds, and the
three repetitions are genuinely different partitions of the centres.

\paragraph{A bug found and fixed.} The first version used \texttt{scikit-learn}'s
\texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} with \texttt{shuffle=True} and a different seed per repetition.
The plot of fold scores in Stage~4 showed every value exactly three times: with only 23
groups, that splitter sorts the groups by their outcome profile before shuffling has any
effect, so all three ``repetitions'' were the same five folds. The dealing procedure above
replaced it, and the second check now guards against a repeat of the problem.

\subsection{The Cox proportional hazards model}
The \emph{hazard} $h(t)$ is the instantaneous risk of death at time $t$ for someone still
alive at $t$. The Cox model~\cite{cox1972} writes the hazard of a patient with features
$x = (x_1, \dots, x_p)$ as
\begin{equation}
h(t \mid x) = h_0(t)\, \exp\!\left(\beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p\right),
\label{eq:cox}
\end{equation}
where $h_0(t)$ is a baseline hazard shared by everyone and left completely unspecified.
In plain language: every patient's risk curve has the same shape over time, scaled up or
down by a patient-specific factor $\exp(\beta^\top x)$. The ratio of two patients' hazards
therefore does not depend on $t$ (the \emph{proportional hazards} assumption), and
$e^{\beta_k}$ is the \emph{hazard ratio} (HR) for a one-unit increase in $x_k$ with the
other features held fixed. The linear predictor $\eta = \beta^\top x$ is exactly the risk
score the challenge asks for.

The coefficients are found by maximising the \emph{partial likelihood}
\begin{equation}
L(\beta) = \prod_{i:\ \Delta_i = 1}
\frac{\exp(\beta^\top x_i)}{\sum_{j:\ T_j \ge T_i} \exp(\beta^\top x_j)}.
\label{eq:partial}
\end{equation}
Each factor asks: of all patients still at risk when patient $i$ died, how likely was it
to be patient $i$? Censored patients appear in the denominators while they are at risk,
which is how the model uses them, and $h_0(t)$ cancels out.

\paragraph{Worked example.} TP53 multi-hit has HR 3.21 in the model below, so its
coefficient is $\ln 3.21 = 1.166$. Haemoglobin has HR 0.768 per standard deviation (2.0
g/dL), so $\beta = \ln 0.768 = -0.264$. A patient with TP53 multi-hit whose haemoglobin is
one standard deviation \emph{below} average, all else equal, has
\[
\text{HR} = \exp\!\big(1.166 - (-0.264)\big) = \exp(1.430) = 4.18
= 3.21 \times \frac{1}{0.768},
\]
that is, about four times the instantaneous risk of death of an otherwise average patient.

\subsection{Penalised Cox: the elastic net}
With 84 features, some rare, ordinary maximisation of Equation~\eqref{eq:partial}
over-fits. The elastic net~\cite{zou2005,simon2011} instead maximises
\begin{equation}
\ln L(\beta) \;-\; \alpha\left(\rho \sum_k |\beta_k| + \frac{1-\rho}{2}\sum_k \beta_k^2\right),
\label{eq:enet}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha > 0$ sets the overall strength of the penalty and $\rho \in (0, 1]$ the mix:
$\rho = 1$ is the lasso, which sets many coefficients exactly to zero, and small $\rho$ is
close to ridge regression, which shrinks all coefficients towards zero without removing
them. Features are standardised first so the penalty treats them equally.

\paragraph{Nested cross-validation.} $\alpha$ and $\rho$ must be chosen without looking at
the validation fold. Inside each outer training set, an inner four-fold split, also
grouped by centre, scores 30 values of $\alpha$ for each $\rho \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1\}$; the
best pair is refitted on the whole outer training set and only then applied to the outer
validation fold. Missing values are filled with medians and features are standardised
inside a \texttt{scikit-learn} pipeline, so these steps are also learned only on the data
the model is trained on.

\subsection{Models compared}
All models are evaluated on the same 15 centre-grouped validation folds, with the
censoring curve $\hat G$ of the metric estimated on each fold's training part:
\begin{description}
  \item[A. IPSS-R score] the published score, no fitting (reference).
  \item[B. Benchmark-style Cox] blasts, haemoglobin, platelets and the number of mutations.
        The challenge's benchmark notebook is available only to registered participants;
        this is my reconstruction from the challenge description and may differ in detail.
  \item[C. IPSS-M-style Cox] the five blood values, the IPSS-R cytogenetic category, a

        molecular score~\cite{bernard2022} (including TP53 multi-hit and MLL-PTD), and
        the number of mutated IPSS-M ``residual'' genes capped at 2. These 24 variables
        come from the literature, not from this data. A very small ridge penalty (0.1)
        keeps the fit stable.
  \item[D. Elastic-net Cox] all 84 features except monocytes and the missing flags.
  \item[E. Elastic-net Cox $+$] D plus log monocytes and the six missing flags.
\end{description}

\subsection{Checking proportional hazards}
If the proportional-hazards assumption holds, each coefficient $\beta_k$ is constant over
time. Grambsch and Therneau~\cite{grambsch1994} showed that the scaled Schoenfeld residual
of a death at time $t$, plus $\hat\beta_k$, estimates $\beta_k(t)$; a trend over time
signals a violation. Table~\ref{tab:ph} gives the test for the largest statistics.

\section{Machine-learning survival models (Stage 5)}
\label{sec:trees}
% ==================================================================
Cox models assume that each feature acts the same way at all times (proportional hazards)
and adds to the log hazard without interacting with the others. Tree-based models drop
both assumptions. Two were fitted on the same 84 features as the elastic-net and
evaluated on the same 15 centre-grouped folds.

\subsection{Random survival forest}
A random survival forest~\cite{ishwaran2008} grows many survival trees. Each tree is grown
on a bootstrap sample of patients (drawn with replacement) and, at every split, considers
only a random subset of the features. A split is chosen to separate the survival curves
of the two child nodes as much as possible (by the log-rank statistic of Stage~2). Each
leaf stores the Nelson--Aalen estimate of the cumulative hazard of its patients, and the
forest averages these over trees. The risk score is the predicted cumulative hazard summed
over the observed event times: more expected deaths means higher risk. Settings: 300
trees, at least 15 patients per leaf, and $\sqrt{84} \approx 9$ features tried per split.
These were fixed rather than tuned, because forests are not very sensitive to them and one
fit takes about six seconds.

\subsection{Gradient boosting with a Cox loss}
Gradient boosting~\cite{friedman2001} builds the risk score as a sum of small trees,
\[
\eta(x) = \nu \sum_{m=1}^{M} f_m(x),
\]
added one at a time. Each new tree $f_m$ is fitted to the gradient of the negative Cox
log partial likelihood (Equation~\eqref{eq:partial}) at the current scores. In plain
language, each tree pushes the scores in the direction that most improves the Cox fit,
and the learning rate $\nu = 0.03$ keeps every step small. The XGBoost
implementation~\cite{chen2016} was used, with 80\% of patients and half the features
sampled for each tree and a minimum leaf weight of 10. It handles missing values itself
by learning which branch they should follow. The tree depth (2, 3 or 4) and the number of
trees (50 to 600) were chosen by the same nested, centre-grouped inner cross-validation
as the elastic-net. In the first run, the smallest number of trees offered (100) was chosen
in 7 of 15 folds. A choice at the edge of the grid means the best value might lie beyond
it, so 50 and 150 were added. After that, 50 was never chosen, and the choices spread
between 100 and 300 trees.

\paragraph{Computational reproducibility.} The first version used XGBoost's fast
approximate (``histogram'') split finding with two threads. Run on Linux and on Windows
with the same XGBoost version (3.4.1), every other model in this report gave identical
results, but XGBoost did not: a cross-validated mean of 0.7227 on one machine and 0.7238 on
the other, with different depths chosen in some folds. That difference (0.001) was as large
as XGBoost's whole advantage over the elastic-net at the time. The final version uses exact
split finding on a single thread and averages five models with different random seeds
(42 to 46) at the depth and number of trees chosen by the inner cross-validation. Each
machine now reproduces its own results exactly. A small difference between machines
remains, because the random row and column subsampling relies on random-number routines
of the C++ standard library, which the Windows and Linux compilers implement differently.
It shrank to 0.0002 in every summary (for example, the ensemble of Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}
gains $+0.0032$ over the elastic-net on Windows and $+0.0030$ on Linux, winning 15 of 15
folds on both), and no conclusion depends on it. All numbers for XGBoost and the
ensembles in this report are from the author's Windows run.

\subsection{Rank-averaged ensembles}
The elastic-net, XGBoost and the forest produce risk scores on different scales: a linear
predictor, a log relative risk and a summed cumulative hazard. Because the metric depends
only on the order of the scores, each model's scores were converted to ranks between 0
and 1 within the set of patients being scored, and the ranks were averaged. For example,
for four patients with elastic-net scores $(1.2, -0.3, 0.4, 2.0)$ and XGBoost scores
$(0.9, 0.1, 0.8, 0.5)$, the ranks are $(0.75, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00)$ and
$(1.00, 0.25, 0.75, 0.50)$, and the ensemble score is their average
$(0.875, 0.25, 0.625, 0.75)$. The two models disagree about patients~1 and~4, and the
average splits the difference. Converting one model's scores to ranks leaves its own
C-index unchanged; averaging the ranks of two models produces a new order.

No model was refitted for this step. The out-of-fold predictions of each model, saved for
all three repetitions, were combined inside each validation fold, so the ensembles are
scored on exactly the same 15 folds and remain honest: every prediction still comes from a
model that never saw that patient's centre.

The two-model ensemble E1 is the best and, more importantly, the most \emph{consistent}

ensemble adds only $+0.0006$ on average; its value is mainly that it removes XGBoost's
losing fold and narrows the spread. Adding the forest (E2) does not help. The gains are
small because the models largely agree: inside the validation folds, the average Spearman
correlation of the risk scores is 0.963 between the elastic-net and XGBoost, 0.966 between
the elastic-net and the forest, and 0.964 between XGBoost and the forest. Averaging helps
only where models disagree, and these models disagree on few patients.

\subsection{Permutation importance}
Gain (Figure~\ref{fig:xgbimp}) and coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:enet}) describe what a
model uses on its training data. Permutation importance~\cite{breiman2001} instead
measures what the model loses on \emph{unseen} patients when a kind of information is
scrambled. In each of the five folds of the first repetition, the elastic-net and XGBoost
were fitted on the training part. Then, in the validation fold, the columns of one feature
group were shuffled together among the patients (five times), and the drop in IPCW
C-index was recorded. Features were shuffled in groups because related features (the 18
karyotype columns, for instance) carry overlapping information, and shuffling one alone
would barely hurt while its partners still hold the information.

\end{figure}

Three findings stand out (Figure~\ref{fig:perm}, Table~\ref{tab:perm}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mutation burden matters most} for both models: the number of mutations and
        of mutated genes, the maximum VAF and the number of truncating mutations, all
        counted on the shared gene panel. Scrambling them costs about 0.04, more than twice
        any other group. This supports the decision in Stage~3 to count only genes
        sequenced in both centres: these counts are the models' strongest signal.
  \item \textbf{The two models use TP53 differently.} The elastic-net loses 0.016 without
        the TP53 group, XGBoost only 0.007. The lesion frequencies of Stage~3 are separate
        prevalences and cannot show whether TP53 and complex karyotypes occur in the
        \emph{same} patients, so this was checked directly (Table~\ref{tab:tp53co}). They
        do: 88.5\% of TP53 multi-hit patients have a complex karyotype, against 4.7\% of
        the others, and 60.3\% of all complex karyotypes are TP53 multi-hit. A tree can
        build rules such as ``complex karyotype \emph{and} high VAF'' that recover most of
        the TP53 signal when the TP53 columns are shuffled; a linear model adds effects and
        relies on the TP53 columns directly. The smaller drop for XGBoost means its TP53
        group is less \emph{irreplaceable}, not that TP53 matters less. TP53 multi-hit
        patients do not carry more mutated genes (a median of 2 in both groups), so the
        overlap runs through the karyotype and a higher VAF, not through mutation burden.
  \item \textbf{Blasts look unimportant only because of overlap.} Bone-marrow blasts were
        the strongest single raw variable in Stage~2, yet shuffling them alone costs
        almost nothing. The IPSS-R score already contains the blast category, so the
        information survives in another group. Permutation importance measures what is
        \emph{unique} to a group, not how prognostic it is on its own. Because the IPSS-R
        score also contains haemoglobin, platelets, neutrophils and cytogenetics, the drop
        from shuffling blasts and the IPSS-R score together cannot be found by adding
        their separate drops; it would need a joint permutation.
\end{enumerate}
White cells and neutrophils are used by XGBoost (0.004) but not by the elastic-net, a hint
of a non-linear effect that a straight-line Cox term cannot capture.

\subsection{Final model choice}
The final model was chosen by paired cross-validation, before its public score was known:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Final choice: E1, the rank average of the elastic-net Cox and the
        seed-averaged XGBoost Cox} (cross-validated IPCW C-index $0.725 \pm 0.021$). It has
        the highest mean, it wins all 15 folds against the elastic-net, and it has the
        smallest paired spread of any model.
  \item It combines an interpretable linear model, whose hazard ratios and coefficients are
        reported in Stage~4, with a tree model that can use non-linear and short-term
        effects.
  \item The improvement over the elastic-net alone is small (+0.003). The conclusions of
        this report do not depend on it: most of the predictive power comes from the
        feature engineering, which added +0.028 over the benchmark variables.
\end{itemize}

their cross-validated means, by 0.022 to 0.033. The C-index depends on the population as
well as on the model: a population with a wider range of risk contains more pairs that are
easy to order. The test centre has many very high-risk patients (a third are IPSS-R very
high) alongside low-risk ones. Its censoring pattern and the size of the public subset
also affect the score, so the case mix is a plausible explanation rather than a proven one.
The offset is not the same for every model, and the order of models changes between
cross-validation and the test centre. That is why models were chosen by paired
cross-validation and the leaderboard was used only as a check.

\subsection{The IPSS-M as benchmark}
The Molecular International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-M)~\cite{bernard2022} is the
current clinical standard for myelodysplastic syndromes. It combines haemoglobin,
platelets, blasts, the IPSS-R cytogenetic category, TP53 multi-hit status, MLL-PTD, FLT3
and 17 other genes with published weights:
\begin{equation}
\text{IPSS-M} = \frac{1}{\ln 2}\sum_{k} \beta_k\,\left(x_k - \mu_k\right),
\label{eq:ipssm}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_k$ is the published coefficient of variable $k$ and $\mu_k$ its reference
mean. Blasts are capped at 20\% and divided by 5, platelets capped at 250 G/L and divided
by 100, and the residual genes enter as their count capped at 2. The weights were taken
from the authors' open-source calculator~\cite{ipssmcalc}. A missing input receives its
reference mean, so it contributes nothing; only published constants are used, and nothing
is learned from these data. The implementation was checked against the official
calculator on 300 training patients with complete data: all 300 scores agreed exactly.
One deviation is unavoidable: copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity of TP53 is not
available, so a single TP53 mutation counts as multi-hit only with VAF above 0.55 or a 17p
loss.

\paragraph{An important caveat.} The IPSS-M was developed on 2{,}957 patients of the
International Working Group for Prognosis in MDS, an international consortium that very
probably overlaps with this challenge's 23 training centres (the challenge is run with the
institute of the IPSS-M's first author). If so, its weights were partly fitted to these
patients, and its scores on the training data are partly in-sample and therefore
optimistic. Its fair comparison is on the test centre (Section~\ref{sec:ipssmtest}).

\subsection{Confidence intervals}
The 15 cross-validation scores are not independent: the three repetitions reuse the same
patients, and the training sets of different folds overlap. The naive standard error
$s/\sqrt{J}$ (with $J = 15$ folds and $s$ the standard deviation across folds) is
therefore too small. The corrected resampled $t$-test of Nadeau and
Bengio~\cite{nadeau2003} inflates the variance to
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}} = \left(\frac{1}{J} + \frac{n_{\text{valid}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\right) s^2
= \left(\frac{1}{15} + 0.257\right) s^2 = 0.323\, s^2,
\]
where $0.257$ is the average ratio of validation to training patients. Since
$\sqrt{0.323/0.067} = 2.2$, the corrected standard error is about 2.2 times the naive one.
Intervals use the $t$ distribution with 14 degrees of freedom ($t_{0.975} = 2.145$).

\subsection{Brier score and calibration}
The C-index only judges the ranking. A clinician also needs predicted
\emph{probabilities} to be right: of 100 patients given a 60\% chance of surviving three
years, about 60 should survive. Three models giving survival probabilities were compared
on the five folds of the first repetition: a Kaplan--Meier curve with no features (a
reference), the IPSS-M recalibrated, and the elastic-net Cox with its Breslow baseline
survival. ``Recalibrated'' means a one-variable Cox model on the IPSS-M score, fitted on
each training part: the IPSS-M weights stay published, and only the link from score to
probability is estimated. The Brier score~\cite{graf1999} at time $t$ is the average
squared difference between the predicted survival probability and the observed status
(alive or dead) at $t$, weighted by IPCW; the integrated Brier score averages it over 0.25
to 6 years. Lower is better.

\subsection{The IPSS-M on the test centre}
\label{sec:ipssmtest}
The IPSS-M score of the 1{,}193 test patients was uploaded to the leaderboard on
25~October 2026 (file \texttt{stage8\_ipssm\_score.csv}). It scored \textbf{0.7255}, against
0.7552 for the ensemble (Table~\ref{tab:lb}). The gap at the test centre,
\[
0.7552 - 0.7255 = 0.0297,
\]
is six times the cross-validated gap of 0.0047. Every fitted model gained between 0.022 and
0.033 from cross-validation to the test centre; the IPSS-M gained only 0.005. Even the
IPSS-M-style Cox model, which uses the same variables as the IPSS-M but fits its own
weights, scored higher on the test centre (0.7374 against 0.7255), although it was lower in
cross-validation (0.716 against 0.720).

Before explaining the gap, three candidate explanations were tested on the training data,
where the outcomes are known (script \texttt{15\_ipssm\_external\_check.py},
Table~\ref{tab:ipssmchecks}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Case mix.} The test centre is much sicker: 33\% of its patients are IPSS-R
        very high risk, against 11\% in training. Each validation fold was resampled, 20
        times without replacement, so that its mix of the five IPSS-R risk groups matched the
        test centre. The gap stayed at $+0.005$. A sicker case mix does not, by itself,
        widen the gap.
  \item \textbf{Missing clinical values.} Blood counts and blasts are missing for about
        9.5\% of test patients against 2.5\% in training. Both models replace a missing
        value with a typical one (the IPSS-M its reference mean, the elastic-net the training
        median). Removing these values for an extra 7\% of each validation fold lowered the
        elastic-net by 0.0023 and the IPSS-M by 0.0026: the gap widened by only 0.0003.
  \item \textbf{Centre-to-centre variation.} The gap was scored inside each of the eight
        training centres with at least 100 patients, using out-of-fold predictions so that
        no model had seen the centre it was scored on. It ranges from $-0.014$ (ROM) to
        $+0.019$ (DUS), with a standard deviation of 0.011 (Figure~\ref{fig:gapcentre}). In
        three of the eight centres, including the largest (KI, 820 patients), the IPSS-M is
        ahead.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What this means.} The test-centre gap is real in the sense that neither of the
two obvious differences between the centres explains it. It lies about 2.4 standard
deviations above the average training-centre gap ($+0.003$), larger than any training
centre showed. Two readings are consistent with the evidence. The first is the caveat
above: if the IPSS-M was developed partly on the training centres, its cross-validated
score is optimistic, and a centre outside its development cohort would reveal its true
level; the IPSS-M-style
Cox overtaking it on the test centre fits this reading. The second is simply that the gap
varies a lot from hospital to hospital, and this hospital is at the far end. One centre
cannot separate the two. The public score also uses only part of the test centre, and
Challenge Data gives no confidence interval for it, so the size of the gap is itself
uncertain.

\subsection{Summary of Stage 8}
Against the IPSS-R, every fitted model is clearly and significantly better. Against the
IPSS-M, a published score with no fitting, they are statistically indistinguishable in
cross-validation, and the IPSS-M is slightly better calibrated. On the unseen test centre,
which is not among the training centres, the ensemble was ahead by 0.030. Case mix and
missing values do not explain this, but it rests on one centre, so it is evidence that the
fitted models transfer better, not proof.

% ==================================================================
\section{Discussion}
% ==================================================================

\paragraph{What mattered.} The largest gain in this project came from the features, not from
the algorithm. Moving from the four benchmark variables to 24 variables chosen from the
clinical literature (IPSS-R cytogenetics, IPSS-M genes, TP53 multi-hit, MLL-PTD) added
0.028 in cross-validation and won 14 of 15 folds. Every later step, namely 84 penalised
features, boosted trees and the ensemble, added at most 0.006 more. Permutation importance
confirms the point: the strongest signal is the mutation burden, a simple count that only
became comparable across centres once it was restricted to the shared gene panel.
The published IPSS-M, which encodes the same expert knowledge in fixed weights, performs
as well as every fitted model in cross-validation (Section~\ref{sec:stage8}), where it may
be favoured by having been developed partly on the same patients. On the test centre, which
is not among the training centres, it fell 0.030 behind the ensemble, a gap that neither case mix nor missing values
explains (Section~\ref{sec:ipssmtest}). The honest conclusion is that this work reproduces
the performance of the current clinical standard from the challenge data, with transparent
and validated code, and may transfer better to a new hospital, but a single external centre
cannot establish that it surpasses it.

\paragraph{Validation that matches the test.} The test set is a hospital never seen in
training, so every model was validated on held-out hospitals and compared fold by fold on
the same 15 folds. The paired comparison removes the large differences in difficulty
between folds (the IPSS-R score alone ranges from 0.624 to 0.729 across folds) and leaves
each model's own advantage. Hyperparameters were always chosen by an inner,
centre-grouped split, so no validation fold was used twice.

\paragraph{Checking the details.} Three problems were found only by looking closely at
intermediate results, and each would otherwise have biased the conclusions. The three
``random'' repetitions of cross-validation were identical, visible as every fold score
appearing three times. A tuning grid was too narrow, with the smallest value chosen most
often. And XGBoost gave different results on Windows and Linux. A fourth case was a
hypothesis rather than a bug: the explanation that trees fail on high-risk patients was
tested on the cross-validation predictions and rejected.

\paragraph{Clinical sense.} The fitted hazard ratios agree with established knowledge:
TP53 multi-hit (HR 3.21), MLL partial tandem duplication (2.05) and FLT3 mutation (1.65)
carry the highest risk, and haemoglobin and platelets are protective. Two findings show why
adjusted and unadjusted analyses differ. \textit{SF3B1} is protective on its own but
neutral once blasts, karyotype and blood counts are included, because \textit{SF3B1}
patients are favoured on most of those. And \textit{NPM1} is harmful in the first months but
protective later, a violation of proportional hazards that its single Cox coefficient hides.

\section{Limitations}
% ==================================================================
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Overlap with the IPSS-M cohort.} The training data very probably overlap
        with the cohort on which the IPSS-M was developed. Its cross-validated scores here
        may be optimistic, and the data are not new to the field. Whether this explains the
        IPSS-M's weaker test-centre score cannot be checked without knowing which centres
        were in its development cohort.
  \item \textbf{One external centre.} The test set comes from a single hospital, and the
        public score uses only part of it. Conclusions about how the models transfer rest
        on this one centre. The ensemble's lead over the IPSS-M varies from $-0.014$ to
        $+0.019$ across training centres, so a single centre's gap is a noisy measurement.
        The private leaderboard, updated on 15~June and 15~December, will give a second,
        larger look at the same centre.
  \item \textbf{Case-mix shift.} The test centre is much sicker than the training centres.
        The models were not adapted to it, for example by reweighting training patients to
        resemble the test centre.
  \item \textbf{Measurement differences.} The gene panels differ between centres, so 60
        training genes and 598 training mutations were not used. Monocyte counts were
        dropped because they are mostly missing at the test centre. Laboratory calibration
        differences cannot be checked from the data.
  \item \textbf{The karyotype parser} is a set of hand-written rules tested on 25 cases and
        cross-checked against a simpler rule. It is not a validated ISCN parser, and rare
        or badly written karyotypes may be misread.
  \item \textbf{Censoring.} The metric and the Cox models assume that censoring is unrelated
        to the patient's prognosis. Follow-up practices that differ between centres could
        violate this.
  \item \textbf{Missing information.} There is no information on treatment (for example,
        stem cell transplantation), age, or disease subtype, all of which affect survival.
  \item \textbf{The benchmark model} was reconstructed from the challenge description, since
        the benchmark notebook was not available.
  \item \textbf{Computation.} XGBoost results differ in the fourth decimal place between
        operating systems; all reported numbers come from one machine.
\end{itemize}

% ==================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ==================================================================
For adult myeloid neoplasms, a carefully engineered set of clinical, cytogenetic and
molecular features, validated on held-out hospitals, predicts overall survival with an IPCW
C-index of about 0.72 in cross-validation and 0.755 on an unseen test centre, about 0.05
better than the IPSS-R clinical score. The final model, a rank-averaged ensemble of an
elastic-net Cox and a gradient-boosted Cox model, was chosen by paired cross-validation
before its public score was known, and it was the best submitted model on the test centre.
In cross-validation it does not measurably improve on the published IPSS-M, which reaches
the same discrimination with no fitting and slightly better calibration; on the unseen test
centre it led the IPSS-M by 0.030 (0.7552 against 0.7255), a promising result that a single
centre cannot confirm. The main lessons are that
domain knowledge and a validation design that mirrors the real test mattered far more than
the choice of algorithm, and that a new model should always be compared with the strongest
existing clinical score before any claim of improvement.
